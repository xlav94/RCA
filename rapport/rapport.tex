\begin{abstract}

We study a reinforcement learning approach to portfolio allocation that aims to balance adaptability with explicit
risk control. While classical methods such as Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT) and Post-Modern Portfolio Theory (PMPT)
provide clear frameworks for managing risk, they rely on static optimization and do not adapt to changing market
conditions. On the other hand, many deep reinforcement learning methods offer flexibility but lack a direct connection
to financial principles.

To address this gap, we propose a hybrid framework in which a PPO-based reinforcement learning agent interacts with the
market, while its decisions are passed through a financial optimization layer based on either MPT or PMPT. This design
ensures that portfolio allocations remain consistent with risk-aware objectives, while still benefiting from
data-driven adaptation. The model processes both historical price sequences, using an LSTM-based feature extractor, and
the current portfolio state to generate allocation decisions.

Experiments on a diversified portfolio of 21 assets show that the proposed approach achieves competitive cumulative
returns compared to an equally-weighted benchmark. However, results also highlight significant variability across
training runs, reflecting the sensitivity of deep reinforcement learning methods to initialization and training conditions.

Overall, this work suggests that combining reinforcement learning with simple financial optimization rules is a
promising direction for building more robust and risk-aware portfolio management strategies.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Portfolio management has traditionally been guided by Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), which aims to maximize returns for
a given level of risk through diversification. Post-Modern Portfolio Theory (PMPT) extends this idea by distinguishing
between desirable and undesirable fluctuations, focusing specifically on downside risk rather than overall volatility.
Both approaches, however, rely on static optimization and do not adapt well to the constantly changing nature of
financial markets.

At the same time, recent advances in machine learning, particularly in reinforcement learning, have introduced more
adaptive approaches to portfolio management. However, many of these methods lack a strong grounding in financial theory,
often prioritizing performance without explicitly controlling for risk, leaving a gap between adaptability and disciplined
risk management.

In this project, we study a reinforcement learning agent designed to manage a portfolio composed of a limited number of
financial assets and an initial capital. At each time step, the agent observes the market through a sliding window of
historical price data, enriched with fractional differentiation to retain long-term dependencies while ensuring
stationarity. Based on this information, the agent adjusts the portfolio allocation across assets, and its decisions are
evaluated through a reward function that accounts for both returns and risk.

To bridge the gap between static financial theory and adaptive learning, we introduce a hybrid approach, in which the
agent's decisions are passed through an optimization layer that implements either a Mean-Variance (MPT) or a
Mean-Semi-deviation (PMPT) objective, so that the final allocations explicitly balance expected returns with risk
considerations. This design introduces a separation of responsibilities: the agent focuses on predicting
expected returns, while the optimization layer handles risk management. Without this layer,
the agent would need to learn both tasks simultaneously from reward signals alone, making it a significantly harder problem.
This integration allows us to leverage the adaptability of reinforcement learning while ensuring that the agent's behavior
remains consistent with established financial principles. The learning process is based on the
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, an on-policy actor-critic method in which the actor suggests actions while
the critic evaluates their quality and guides the learning process. PPO's ability to constrain policy updates ensures
stable and progressive improvements without unlearning previously acquired knowledge.

The main objective of this project is twofold: first, to combine recurrent neural networks (RNN/LSTM) and reinforcement
learning to manage a financial portfolio in a sequential decision-making context; and second, to integrate classical financial
optimizers, MPT and PMPT, directly into the agent's decision pipeline, in order to produce allocation strategies that are\
both adaptive and explicitly risk-aware.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work on reinforcement
learning and portfolio optimization. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the proposed architecture, including the
feature engineering pipeline, the PPO agent, and the financial optimization layer. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents
and discusses the experimental results. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses the limitations of our approach. Finally,
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes and outlines directions for future work.

\section{Related Works}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Foundational Financial Theories}
Traditional portfolio optimization methods, such as Markowitz's Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT)
\citep{markowitz1952portfolio}, have been the industry standard for decades to balance risk and return through
diversification. While effective in theory, MPT relies on static rules that often struggle to keep up with the chaotic,
fast-moving reality of today's markets.
Post-Modern Portfolio Theory (PMPT) was introduced by \citet{rom1993postmodern} as a refinement of MPT, replacing
variance with downside deviation as the measure of risk, that is, penalizing only returns that fall below a target
threshold rather than treating upside and downside fluctuations symmetrically. This asymmetric treatment better reflects
the actual preferences of investors and provides a more nuanced framework for portfolio optimization under uncertainty.

With the rise of machine learning, new approaches have emerged to tackle these limitations. \citet{jang2023deep} proposed
integrating Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) with Technical Analysis and Modern Portfolio Theory constraints. Unlike
traditional supervised learning models that merely predict prices, DRL agents learn sequential decision-making policies
directly through interaction with the market environment. This transition from static allocation to dynamic portfolio
management is emphasized in the comprehensive survey by \citet{pippas2025evolution}, which traces the evolution of
reinforcement learning techniques in quantitative finance.

\subsection{Feature Engineering: Fractional Differentiation in Financial
Machine Learning}
A recurring challenge in applying machine learning to financial time series is the stationarity-memory dilemma: raw
price series are non-stationary, yet standard differencing to achieve stationarity, such as computing logarithmic
returns, removes most of the long-range dependencies that carry predictive information. Fractional differentiation
addresses this by applying a non-integer differencing operator $d \in (0, 1)$, producing a series that is stationary
while retaining meaningful historical memory. \citet{stempień2025comparativeanalysisfinancialdata} demonstrated its
empirical relevance on four major stock indices, finding that fractionally differentiated inputs consistently
outperformed logarithmic returns as inputs to a neural network, motivating our use of per-asset ADF-based selection of
the differentiation parameter.

\subsection{Sequential Modeling and Temporal Dependencies}
Addressing the partial observability of financial markets, \citet{li2015recurrent} introduced a hybrid recurrent
reinforcement learning framework integrating RNN-LSTM architectures within a Q-learning setup. Their approach enables
agents to maintain memory of historical market states, capturing temporal dependencies that are critical in stochastic
and non-stationary environments.

\subsection{The Evolution of Deep Reinforcement Learning in Finance}
Building upon classical frameworks, \citet{jang2023deep} combined DRL with MPT constraints using tensor decomposition
techniques to manage high-dimensional financial data within structured risk frameworks. Similarly, \citet{jiang2017deep}
proposed a CNN-based reinforcement learning framework that automates feature extraction from multi-channel price
representations, reducing reliance on handcrafted indicators and improving scalability.

\subsection{Actor-Critic Frameworks}
Modern portfolio optimization strategies predominantly rely on the Actor-Critic architecture, which simultaneously
updates a policy network (Actor) and a value function estimator (Critic). As implemented in the FinRL framework
\citep{liu2020finrl}, in the ensemble strategy proposed by \citet{yang2025ensemble}, and discussed in \citet{jang2023deep},
actor-critic methods provide improved stability and continuous control capabilities. Among these, Proximal Policy
Optimization (PPO) \citep{schulman2017proximal} is particularly well-suited for portfolio management, as it stabilizes training by constraining the
magnitude of each policy update, preventing the agent from unlearning previously acquired knowledge while ensuring
progressive improvement.

\subsection{Risk-Aware Rewards and Responsible Management}
To ensure robust and responsible financial decision-making, recent literature emphasizes risk-aware reward functions.
Both the FinRL framework \citep{liu2020finrl} and the ensemble strategy proposed by \citet{yang2025ensemble} incorporate
market frictions such as transaction costs and financial turbulence indices. These mechanisms penalize excessive
speculation and enable agents to survive extreme market conditions, including financial crashes.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
This section describes the main components of the proposed system: the environment and data pipeline, the feature
engineering process, and the learning architecture, including the integration of the optimization layer and the reward
function design.
Together, these elements form an end-to-end pipeline in which a reinforcement learning agent learns to allocate capital
across a diversified portfolio of financial assets, in a way that balances adaptability and established financial principles.

\subsection{Environment and Data Pipeline}
The portfolio management problem is formulated as a sequential decision-making task within a custom environment
compatible with the \texttt{Gymnasium} interface. The environment simulates the evolution of a portfolio composed of 21
highly capitalized financial assets, spanning 7 sectors, selected to ensure broad sectoral diversification, as detailed
in Table~\ref{tab:portfolio_assets}.
Historical daily opening prices are retrieved via the Yahoo Finance API, covering the period from January 2010 to
February 2026. To ensure chronological integrity and prevent data leakage, the data undergoes a strict time-series
split: the first 80\% of trading days form the training set, while the remaining 20\% are reserved for out-of-sample test set.

At each time step $t$, the agent receives an observation composed of two components: a market history matrix of dimension
$W \times N$, containing the price series of all $N = 21$ assets over a rolling window of $W = 60$ trading days, enriched with
fractionally differentiated features described in Section~\ref{sec:fracdiff}; and a portfolio state vector
$\mathbf{w}_t \in [0, 1]^N$ describing the current allocation weights across assets. The action space is continuous, with
the actor outputting a vector $\mathbf{a}_t \in [-1, 1]^N$ interpreted as expected returns, which is then passed through
a financial optimization layer to produce the final portfolio weights. The reward signal is described in Section~\ref{sec:reward}.

\subsection{Feature Engineering: Fractional Differentiation}
\label{sec:fracdiff}
Raw asset prices present a fundamental challenge for learning models: they are non-stationary, meaning their statistical
properties drift over time, making it difficult for the agent to generalize across market regimes. A naive solution is
to use daily log-returns, which are stationary but discard all long-range memory, and the agent loses any sense of the
broader price trend.

Fractional differentiation offers a principled middle ground, producing a signal that is stationary while retaining
meaningful historical dependencies.

For each asset $i$, the fractionally differentiated series $\tilde{x}_t^{(i)}$ is
computed as a weighted sum of past prices, where weights decay according to a
fractional parameter $d_i$:

\begin{equation}
    \tilde{x}_t^{(i)} = \sum_{k=0}^{L} \binom{d_i}{k} (-1)^k x_{t-k}^{(i)}
\end{equation}

where $L = 50$ is the truncation window and $\binom{d_i}{k}$ are the binomial
coefficients that govern the rate of memory decay. The parameter $d_i$ is selected
independently for each asset by finding the minimum value that passes the Augmented
Dickey-Fuller (ADF) stationarity test on the training set only, ensuring no
information from the test period influences the transformation. The resulting values,
reported in Table~\ref{tab:fracdiff}, reflect the degree of non-stationarity of
each asset: commodities such as GLD and XOM require relatively low differentiation
($d = 0.35$ and $d = 0.30$ respectively), while technology and financial stocks tend
to require stronger transformations, reflecting their more persistent upward trends.

\subsection{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
A key challenge in this work is that the agent must simultaneously process two fundamentally different types of
input: a sequence of past market prices and the current portfolio allocation, and then produce decisions that are
guided by both the data and financial considerations. To address this, we build a architecture with three main parts:
a dual-stream feature extractor, a PPO actor-critic policy network, and a financial optimization layer that converts
the agent’s output into valid portfolio allocations. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} illustrates the full pipeline.

\subsubsection*{Feature Extractor}
To process the structured observation space, we implement a custom feature extractor composed of two parallel streams:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{LSTM stream:} A Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, configured
    with 2 layers and a hidden size of 128, processes the
    $W \times N$ market history matrix to capture temporal dependencies and momentum
    patterns across the 60-day rolling window. The final hidden state
    $h_T \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$ serves as a compact summary of recent market dynamics.

    \item \textbf{MLP stream:} A single linear layer with a ReLU activation
    (\texttt{mlp\_portfolio}) processes the current portfolio state vector
    $\mathbf{w}_t \in [0,1]^N$, encoding the current allocation into the same
    feature space.
\end{itemize}

The outputs of both streams are concatenated to produce a unified feature vector of dimension $128 + N$, which is then
fed into the PPO actor-critic network.
\subsubsection*{PPO Agent}
The learning agent is built around Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an on-policy actor-critic algorithm implemented
via Stable-Baselines3. The actor network takes the combined feature vector as input and outputs a continuous action
$\mathbf{a}_t \in [-1, 1]^N$, interpreted as expected returns $\boldsymbol{\mu}$. The critic network independently
estimates the value of the current state to guide policy updates. PPO constrains the magnitude of each policy update,
preventing the agent from making overly aggressive changes that could destabilize the learned policy.
\subsubsection*{Financial Optimization Layer}
\label{sec:optimizers}
Rather than using the actor's output as portfolio weights directly, we pass it through a financial optimization layer
that solves a constrained optimization problem at each time step. This design ensures that every allocation decision is
by construction consistent with established financial theory. Both optimizers operate over the weight vector $\mathbf{w}
\in \mathbb{R}^N$ subject to the following constraints:

\begin{equation}
    \left\{ w \in \mathbb{R}^n : \sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 1, \quad L \leq w_i \leq U, \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, n\} \right\}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Mean-Variance Optimization (MPT).}
The first optimizer follows Markowitz's mean-variance framework, maximizing expected return penalized by total
portfolio variance:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \max_{w} \quad & w \mu - \frac{1}{2} \lambda (w^T \Sigma w) \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Mean-Semi-deviation Optimization (PMPT).}
The second optimizer replaces the variance penalty with a downside risk term, penalizing only negative portfolio
returns:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \max_{w} \quad & w \mu - \lambda \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \min(0, r_t w)^2 + \epsilon} \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where the variables are defined as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the \textbf{portfolio weights vector}, representing the proportion of capital allocated to each of the $n$ assets.
    \item $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the \textbf{expected returns vector} (output by the PPO Actor).
    \item $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is the \textbf{covariance matrix} of asset returns, representing total risk.
    \item $r_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ denotes the \textbf{realized return vector} at time $t$ within a look-back window of size $N$.
    \item $\lambda > 0$ is the \textbf{risk aversion coefficient}, which scales the penalty for volatility or downside risk.
    \item $L, U$ are the \textbf{lower and upper bounds} for individual asset weights (e.g., $L=0, U=0.2$).
    \item $\epsilon$ is a \textbf{numerical stability constant} ($10^{-4}$) used to ensure the differentiability of the square root in the PMPT objective.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reward Function}
\label{sec:reward}

The reward function is the primary mechanism through which the agent learns what constitutes good behavior. Beyond
simply rewarding returns, it must encode the broader objective of this work: discouraging excessive reallocation and
promoting stable, long-term investment strategies. At each time step $t$, the reward $R_t$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    R_t = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_{i,t} \left( \frac{p_{i,t} - p_{i,t-1}}{p_{i,t-1}} \right)}_{\text{Portfolio Return}} - \underbrace{\lambda \sum_{i=1}^{n} |w_{i,t} - w_{i,t-1}|}_{\text{Transaction Penalty}}
\end{equation}
where the variables are defined as follows:
\begin{align*}
    w_{i,t} &\in [0, 0.2] && \text{portfolio weight assigned to asset } i
                              \text{ at time } t \\
    p_{i,t} &\in \mathbb{R}^+ && \text{opening price of asset } i
                                  \text{ at time } t \\
    \lambda &&& \text{penalty factor}
\end{align*}
The first term measures the portfolio's raw return over the period, computed as the weighted sum of individual asset
returns. The second term penalizes large changes in allocation between consecutive steps, using the $\ell_1$ norm of
the weight difference vector. This penalty serves two purposes: it approximates real-world transaction costs, and it
discourages the kind of aggressive, high-frequency reallocation that would be impractical in live trading. Together,
these two terms directly align the agent's incentives with the responsible risk management objective that motivates
this work: the agent is rewarded for generating returns, but only if it does so through purposeful and stable
allocation decisions.

\subsection{Training}
\label{sec:training}
Training is conducted using 8 parallel environments via \texttt{SubprocVecEnv}, which allows the agent to collect
experience across multiple independent market simulations simultaneously and accelerates convergence. The entire
environment is normalized online using \texttt{VecNormalize}, which standardizes both observations and rewards using a
running mean and variance estimate, stabilizing training across the wide range of asset return magnitudes.

The PPO agent is trained for a total of 10 million timesteps, with model checkpoints saved every 5 million steps to
allow for post-hoc selection of the best policy.

To account for the randomness in reinforcement learning, coming from both weight initialization and environment
sampling, training is repeated across 4 random seeds. Training progress is monitored via TensorBoard, tracking
portfolio returns, allocation weights, and transaction penalties across episodes. All key hyperparameters are
reported in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}.

\subsection{Cumulative Return}

Figure~\ref{fig:cumret_new} compares the mean cumulative return of the PPO agent against an equally-weighted
buy-and-hold benchmark over the 803-day test horizon. The shaded region around the PPO curve represents the standard
deviation across the 4 evaluation seeds, reflecting the variance of the learned policy under different random initializations.

The PPO agent achieves a mean cumulative return of 84.94\% over the test horizon, compared to 80.68\% for the
equally-weighted buy-and-hold benchmark.

The variance across seeds, visible in the shaded region, is nonetheless  non-negligible, particularly in the second
half of the test horizon where the spread widens considerably. This sensitivity to random initialization is an
inherent challenge of deep reinforcement learning and is discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Risk-Adjusted Performance}

To complement the analysis of cumulative returns, we assess the
risk-adjusted performance of the PPO agent using the Sharpe ratio.

The PPO agent achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.707, compared to 1.609 for the
equally-weighted buy-and-hold benchmark. This indicates a slight improvement
in return per unit of risk for the learned policy.

Overall, these results suggest that while the PPO agent outperforms the
benchmark in both absolute and risk-adjusted terms, the magnitude of the
improvement remains relatively limited, highlighting the difficulty of
achieving substantial risk-adjusted gains in financial markets.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Despite encouraging results, there are still a few limitations that affect how reliable and reproducible our findings are.

The main issue is the variation we observe across different random seeds. Even though all four runs use the same model
and hyperparameters, Figure~\ref{fig:cumret_new} shows clear differences in performance. Some seeds end up learning much
better strategies than others. This kind of sensitivity to initialization is common in deep reinforcement learning, but
it makes it harder to draw strong conclusions from a single run.

This variation is made worse by hardware constraints. Training for 10 million timesteps with 8 parallel environments is
computationally expensive, and using \texttt{SubprocVecEnv} introduces additional randomness due to parallel execution.
As a result, runs are not perfectly reproducible, even when using fixed seeds.

Another limitation concerns the portfolio weight constraints imposed on the financial optimization layer. Individual
asset weights are bounded between $L = 0$ and $U = 0.2$, capping any single position at 20\% of the portfolio. The
choice of these bounds was not systematically tuned, and different values may lead to meaningfully different
performance profiles.

Finally, the long training time limited how much hyperparameter tuning we could do. Parameters such as the risk aversion
coefficient $\lambda$, the observation window size $W$, and the learning rate were chosen based on a few initial
experiments rather than a thorough search. This means that better configurations may exist but were not explored.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This work explored the combination of deep reinforcement learning and principled financial optimization for portfolio
allocation. By embedding a Mean-Variance or  Mean-Semi-deviation optimization layer directly into the agent's action
pipeline, we ensure that every allocation decision is by construction risk-aware, while still allowing the agent to adapt
to changing market conditions through trial and error. The result is a framework that bridges the gap between the static
discipline of classical financial theory and the flexibility of modern reinforcement learning.

Over an 803-day out-of-sample test horizon, the PPO agent achieves a mean cumulative return of 84.94\%, compared to
80.68\% for the equally-weighted buy-and-hold benchmark. These results suggest that the proposed hybrid
approach is a promising direction for building portfolio management systems that are both adaptive and financially
grounded.

That said, the sensitivity of the learned policy to random initialization remains a significant limitation, and the lack
of systematic hyperparameter tuning leaves open the question of how much further the approach can be pushed. Future work
should focus on improving training stability across seeds, exploring alternative deep reinforcement learning algorithms
such as SAC or TD3, and extending the framework to incorporate richer market signals or alternative risk objectives such
as the Black-Litterman model.
